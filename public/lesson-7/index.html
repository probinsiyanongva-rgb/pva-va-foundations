Lesson 7 of 8

Learn → Practice → Prove → Grow

✓ Completed

You now have a basic picture of VA work and the different directions you can explore.

There's one more important question:

How do I actually become capable of doing the work?

Knowing what a VA does is different from being able to do the work.

A useful way to think about skill development is:

Learn → Practice → Prove → Grow

These four steps will appear throughout the PVA learning journey.

1. Learn

First, you need to understand what you're trying to do.

This might mean learning:

  • a computer skill
  • a software tool
  • a process
  • a type of task
  • a work procedure
  • a communication method
  • a specialized skill

For example, suppose you need to learn how to organize files.

You might first learn:

  • what folders are
  • how files are named
  • how files are moved
  • how folders are organized
  • how files are shared

At this stage, you are building understanding.

But understanding alone isn't enough.

2. Practice

Once you understand something, you need to use it.

This is where practice comes in.

You might:

  • organize a sample folder
  • rename sample files
  • create a spreadsheet
  • respond to a practice customer message
  • follow a sample procedure
  • complete a simulated client task

Practice gives you a chance to work through the task without the pressure of a real client depending on you.

You will probably make mistakes.

That's part of practice.

The useful question is:

What did I get wrong, and what should I do differently next time?

3. Prove

Eventually, you need some way to show what you can actually do.

That's where proof of skill becomes useful.

Proof might be:

  • a completed practice task
  • a sample document
  • a spreadsheet you've prepared
  • a sample customer support response
  • a portfolio piece
  • a project you've completed
  • a work sample created from a realistic scenario

The important part is that the proof should demonstrate a skill.

For example:

Saying:

“I know how to use spreadsheets.”

is a claim.

Showing a well-organized spreadsheet you've actually created gives someone something they can look at.

4. Grow

Once you can perform a task and show your work, you're not finished learning.

You can continue improving.

You may:

  • work faster
  • make fewer errors
  • handle more complicated tasks
  • learn another tool
  • understand a client's process better
  • take on more responsibility
  • develop a specialization

You may also discover that a particular type of work isn't for you.

That's useful information too.

Your direction can change as you gain experience.

Why the Four Steps Matter

Consider two people.

Person A

Has watched several tutorials about spreadsheets.

They understand terms such as:

  • rows
  • columns
  • formulas
  • filters

But they've rarely used a spreadsheet themselves.

Person B

Has learned the basics, practiced organizing several datasets, corrected mistakes, and completed realistic spreadsheet tasks.

Both may say:

“I know spreadsheets.”

But their actual experience is different.

Learning gives you understanding.

Practice gives you experience using the skill.

Proof gives you something concrete to show.

Growth helps you become more capable over time.

A Simple Example

Let's use a basic administrative task.

Task:

“Please organize this list of customer information into a spreadsheet.”

Learn

You learn:

  • how the spreadsheet is structured
  • how to enter information
  • how columns should be organized
  • how to check your entries

Practice

You work with a sample customer list.

You organize it and check your work.

You notice that some information is in the wrong columns.

You correct it.

Prove

You complete another realistic sample task and save the finished spreadsheet as a work sample.

Grow

Later, you learn how to:

  • clean duplicate records
  • sort and filter information
  • use basic formulas
  • create simple reports

The skill has developed beyond the first task.

Practice Is More Than Repeating Clicks

Good practice should help you think about the work.

For example, don't just practice:

“How do I sort a spreadsheet?”

Also practice:

“When should I sort this information, and what should I check afterward?”

Don't just practice:

“How do I send an email?”

Also practice:

“What information should this email contain, who should receive it, and what does the client expect me to communicate?”

The goal is to connect the tool with the task.

Mistakes Are Information

During practice, you may:

  • put information in the wrong place
  • miss an instruction
  • use the wrong file
  • forget a deadline
  • make a formatting mistake
  • misunderstand what the client wanted

When this happens, don't simply repeat the task.

Find out why the mistake happened.

Maybe you:

  • didn't read the full instruction
  • didn't understand a term
  • skipped a checking step
  • didn't ask a question when something was unclear
  • haven't practiced the tool enough

Once you know the reason, you know what to work on.

You Don't Need to Prove Everything

Not every skill needs a separate portfolio piece.

For example, you don't necessarily need a portfolio item showing that you know how to:

  • rename a file
  • create a folder
  • attach a document to an email

These are basic abilities that may simply become part of your normal work.

Proof becomes more useful when you're trying to demonstrate a meaningful skill or type of work.

Later in the PVA journey, you'll learn more about building proof of skill.

The Four Steps Can Repeat

These aren't four steps that you complete once and never revisit.

You may go through the cycle many times.

For example:

  1. Learn
    You learn basic spreadsheet functions.
  2. Practice
    You use them in sample tasks.
  3. Prove
    You create a useful spreadsheet work sample.
  4. Grow
    You learn more advanced spreadsheet skills.
  5. Learn again
    You discover another tool or process.

And the cycle continues.

What Happens When You Start Real Work?

Real client work adds another layer.

You may discover that a task you've practiced is different when:

  • the client has a specific process
  • the deadline is real
  • the information is confidential
  • the instructions are incomplete
  • the client asks for a revision
  • several tasks have different priorities

That's why practice should gradually become more realistic.

The goal is to prepare yourself for the way work actually happens.

Activity

Where Are You?

Think about one skill you already have or are currently learning.

For example: Spreadsheet basics

Write a short answer for each.

You don't need a perfect answer.

The exercise is simply to see where you currently are.

A Useful Way to Look at Your Progress

You might be at different stages with different skills.

For example:

Skill Stage
Email Practice
File management Learn
Spreadsheets Practice
Customer communication Prove
Amazon Seller Central Learn
Social media scheduling Grow

That's normal.

You don't have to be at the same stage with every skill.

The Goal Is Capability

As you continue through PVA, the goal isn't simply to collect lessons or certificates.

You want to reach the point where you can say:

“I understand the task, I can do it, I can check my work, and I can show what I've done.”

That's a much more useful form of progress.

What You Should Know After This Lesson

You should now understand the basic progression:

Learn

Understand the skill, tool, or process.

Practice

Use it repeatedly through realistic tasks.

Prove

Create evidence that demonstrates what you can do.

Grow

Improve the skill, take on more complex work, and continue learning.

You'll move through these stages many times as you develop your VA skills.
